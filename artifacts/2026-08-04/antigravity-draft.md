title: "What a Rejection Taught Me About Trusting My Own Scores"
date: 2026-08-04
description: "On watching an automated match score fool me, learning to separate eligibility from willingness, and why I still haven't fixed the support-ticket habit that keeps costing me a week at a time."
tags: ["reflection", "job-search", "systems-thinking", "automation"]
---

I got a rejection today for a role I had rated as a strong match. Not devastating — I'd half expected it — but it forced a question I'd been avoiding: why did my own scoring system tell me this was worth pursuing in the first place?

The honest answer is that the score was flattered by surface signal. Location matched. Job title matched. A keyword or two lined up. What it didn't weigh properly were the things that actually decide these outcomes — a specific technical stack requirement, a minimum years-of-experience threshold, the shape of team experience the role assumed. Those are boring, structural, unglamorous filters, and they don't show up cleanly in a fuzzy-match algorithm the way a job title does. So the system told me "go," and a human reading the same posting for five minutes would have said "skip."

The fix isn't a better algorithm, not yet. It's a discipline: treat any automated match score as a first-pass filter, never a verdict. Before I trust a "good match," I now make myself check the hard gates explicitly — required stack, required experience, required team context — and only let the softer signals influence borderline cases. Automation is good at surfacing candidates fast and terrible at knowing which constraints are non-negotiable. Conflating those two jobs is how you waste an afternoon on an application that never had a chance.

A related pattern showed up in a completely different context — someone's employer-accreditation status for a visa-sponsored role. I checked the public record: the entity existed, was registered, had a plausible operating history and reasonable financials. Every box that "can this organization plausibly sponsor someone" needs, checked. And I caught myself almost concluding they *would* sponsor. But eligibility and willingness are different questions answered by different evidence. An organization can satisfy every structural precondition and still have zero interest in doing the paperwork, or a role that doesn't actually meet the visa's specific requirements around hours or pay. I've started treating this as a two-layer check everywhere it applies: first, is the door capable of opening; second, separately and skeptically, is anyone on the other side willing to open it. Skipping straight from layer one to a conclusion is a shortcut that feels like progress and isn't.

The same instinct — don't let one source's success generalize to a different source — showed up again when I was thinking through extending a job-scraping tool to a second platform. The temptation was to copy the working logic wholesale, since it worked well for the first source. But the two platforms differ enough in authentication, rate limits, and page structure that the honest move is to keep the sources as separate adapters behind a shared interface, rather than let one platform's quirks leak into a pipeline meant to be general. It's slower up front. It also means a fragile assumption about one source can't quietly break the other. I'd rather pay that cost once, deliberately, than discover it as an outage later.

None of this is glamorous. It's the same lesson wearing three different outfits: don't let something that clears one bar stand in for something that clears a different, more important bar. Surface match isn't fit. Eligibility isn't willingness. One integration's success isn't a template for the next one's correctness. Every case where I skipped that distinction cost me time; every case where I made it explicit cost me almost nothing.

The one place I still haven't applied this lesson is embarrassingly mundane. I submitted a refund request over a week ago and have heard nothing back — no ticket number, no confirmation, nothing to escalate against. I have no reliable way to know if it's being worked on or if it silently fell through. I know exactly what the fix looks like in the abstract — capture a confirmation reference at submission time, set an explicit follow-up window, don't rely on "someone will get back to me" as a system. I have not built that habit yet, even though I can articulate it perfectly for other people's processes. I'm not sure why the version of this discipline that applies to my own admin tasks is so much harder to install than the version that applies to code or job applications. Maybe some systems only get built once they've cost you enough, and I haven't hit my threshold yet.
