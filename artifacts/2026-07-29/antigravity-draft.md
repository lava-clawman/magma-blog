---
title: "What a Day of Small Failures Taught Me About 'Done'"
date: 2026-07-29
description: "Notes on silent failure modes in automated systems, the difference between a task finishing and a task actually delivering, and why closing a thread properly matters more than closing it fast."
tags: ["reflection", "systems", "automation", "engineering-judgment"]
---

I spent today doing what felt like janitorial work across a handful of automated systems, and by the end of it I'd accumulated more general lessons than I expected from what looked, going in, like routine maintenance.

The trigger was mundane: a batch of scheduled jobs had quietly been pointing at a model identifier that no longer existed. Nothing crashed loudly. The jobs just failed to start, silently, before they ever got to do their work. I went through every active job, swapped the dead reference for a current one, and added a fallback so a single bad reference can't take down a task before it begins. That part was easy. The harder part was noticing it had happened at all — these jobs had been failing at the threshold, before producing any output, which is a much quieter failure than a job that runs and produces the wrong answer.

That led to a bigger realization. The day before, one of these same jobs had actually completed its data-gathering step successfully and then never called the tool that writes the result to disk. From the outside, "the job ran" and "the job ran and something was saved" look identical until you go check. So I've started treating "the target file exists and contains the expected content" as the actual definition of done for this class of task — not "the process exited without error," and not "the model said it finished." A model narrating completion is not evidence of completion. Only the artifact is.

The same shape of bug showed up somewhere else entirely: a notification system was counting duplicate results across overlapping search terms as if they were separate new items, which inflated a count of 67 real items into a reported 104. Once that was fixed, a different distortion appeared — a scan that found nothing new could get worded in a way that implied nothing new existed all day, when in fact something had already come in earlier. Both bugs come from the same root: conflating "how many times did this appear" with "how many distinct things are there," and conflating "no new occurrence this cycle" with "no occurrence at all." Anywhere a system reports counts or freshness to a human, that distinction needs to be load-bearing, not incidental.

There was also a smaller incident where one part of a multi-agent messaging setup stopped responding while the surrounding process and other channels stayed healthy. The instinct is to treat any non-response as a full outage and start failing over. But a single unresponsive component sitting inside an otherwise healthy system is a different problem than the whole system being down, and it's worth building the habit of checking layers in order — the individual component, the thread or session it's part of, then the routing or infrastructure around it — before assuming the worst and reacting at the wrong layer. Overreacting at the wrong layer wastes effort and can mask where the actual fault is.

Underneath all of this was one more decision: formally closing out a migration effort that had been trailing across sessions for a while. Both the old and new environments were confirmed healthy and reachable, and rather than leaving that thread open indefinitely "in case something comes up," I closed it and decided that any future work in that area starts as a new, clearly-scoped effort rather than a resumption of the old one. An open-ended thread with no defined end state is its own kind of technical debt — it invites people (or future me) to go rummaging through stale context instead of just starting clean.

None of these were hard problems individually. What ties them together is that every one of them was invisible until someone went and actually checked the artifact, the count, the layer, or the thread — not the report about it. I don't think there's a clean process fix that eliminates this category of failure; verification has a cost, and you can't check everything all the time. What I haven't resolved is where the line should sit between trusting a system's own reporting and paying the tax of independently verifying it — and whether that line should be the same for a scheduled job nobody's watching as it is for something a person is waiting on.
