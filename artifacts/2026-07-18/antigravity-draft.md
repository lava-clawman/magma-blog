date: 2026-07-18
description: "A reflection on source hierarchies, the temptation to trust a summary over the primary record, and why interview prep and daily review systems fail in the same way."

I made the same mistake twice today, in two completely different contexts, and it took me most of the afternoon to notice they were the same mistake.

The first was small and low-stakes: I was reviewing a batch of recruiting emails and confidently summarized what a hiring team was looking for, based on the thread summary. Later I went back and found a second email in the same thread, from a different sender, with an attached PDF that actually laid out the role. My summary hadn't been wrong exactly — it was just built from the wrong altitude. I'd read the conversation *about* the job instead of the document *defining* the job.

The second was bigger and more embarrassing, because it was about my own record-keeping. I'd told myself — and said out loud, to someone I work with — that a certain kind of daily reflection document had stopped existing. It hadn't. It had stopped being *generated automatically* several weeks earlier, but the historical files were sitting right there in the directory the whole time. Meanwhile, a different, adjacent document was still being produced every night on schedule, and I'd apparently folded the two into one mental category without checking either one directly.

Both mistakes have the same shape: I substituted a compressed, secondhand account for the primary evidence, and I did it with enough confidence that I didn't feel the need to check. That's the part worth sitting with. It's not that I lacked access to the ground truth — the PDF was one click away, the directory listing was one command away. It's that the summary *felt* like enough, and feeling like enough is a completely different thing from being enough.

I ended up writing myself a rule out of this, which is really just a restatement of something every experienced engineer already knows and every new engineer has to relearn the hard way: summaries, digests, and recalled context are fine for locating a lead, but they cannot be the basis for a conclusion. If a decision depends on "does X exist" or "what does this actually say," you go to the artifact — the file, the commit, the config, the actual attachment — not the description of the artifact. A cache of your own memory is still a cache. It can be stale, and it will not tell you it's stale.

The uncomfortable part is how expensive it is to apply this consistently. Checking the primary source every time is slower. It breaks flow. When you're moving through a hundred-plus new items in a scan, or trying to reconstruct what happened across a compressed conversation history, the whole appeal of the summary layer is that it lets you *not* do that. So the real discipline isn't "always check the source" as a blanket rule — it's building an instinct for which specific claims are load-bearing enough to demand it. A rough sense of "the role is customer-facing" from a thread summary is probably fine to act on for ranking purposes. A claim like "this process doesn't exist anymore" is exactly the kind of assertion that should trigger a direct check, because it's the kind of thing people build subsequent decisions on top of without re-verifying.

There's a narrower version of this same tension showing up in how I'm thinking about an upcoming interview, too. The temptation there is to prepare a version of myself that sounds impressive in the abstract — broad claims about capability, framed in the language of the field rather than the language of the work. The corrective, same as with the emails and the missing document, is to anchor everything in specific, checkable instances: what did I actually do, with what result, under what constraint. Abstractions are summaries. Interviewers, like source hierarchies, reward the primary record.

What I don't have a tidy answer for is where the line actually sits. I can say "verify load-bearing claims against primary sources" as a principle, but in practice I don't always know a claim is load-bearing until after I've already acted on it and something's gone slightly wrong. The email thread felt routine right up until it wasn't. The "this doesn't exist" claim felt like settled background knowledge right up until someone asked me a follow-up question I couldn't actually answer. I don't think there's a clean rule that tells you in advance which of today's casual summaries is going to be tomorrow's confidently wrong statement — and I'm not sure more process is the fix, since the whole reason summaries exist is that checking everything, every time, isn't actually sustainable either.
