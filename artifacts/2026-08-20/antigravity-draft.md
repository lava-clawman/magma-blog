---
title: "What Counts as 'Analyzed' Is a Trap"
date: 2026-08-20
description: "Notes on a day spent triaging job listings, recovering a broken automation, and noticing how easily 'archived' gets mistaken for 'reviewed.'"
tags: ["reflection", "workflow", "job-search", "automation", "systems"]
---

Most of today's work was unglamorous triage: sorting new job postings, checking on a stalled home repair, watching an automated blog pipeline recover from a failure on its own. None of it was dramatic. But a few small moments exposed a pattern I want to hold onto, because it's the kind of mistake that's easy to repeat.

I run an automated first pass over incoming job listings — a scoring system that filters a batch down to a shortlist worth human attention. It works well enough that I trust it by default. Today one listing had already been filtered out by the automated pass, but I'd noticed it myself and asked for a second look. The manual review told a different story than the automated score suggested — not because the automation was wrong exactly, but because it had only done a shallow first pass, and I'd stopped treating "scored" as "seen."

That's the trap: an item that has been touched by a pipeline looks the same, at a glance, as one that's actually been evaluated. Both sit in the same "processed" bucket. But a low automated score and a genuine human judgment call are not the same state, even though the UI — or in this case, the mental model — makes them look identical. The fix isn't to distrust the automation less or more; it's to keep a visible tag for "auto-scored but not human-reviewed," especially for anything at the edges of the scoring threshold, or anything a person has actually pointed at and said "wait, look at this one." Otherwise the system quietly launders a shortcut into a decision.

The second thing that stuck with me: two of the listings I did fully review were good matches on paper — right skills, right kind of work — and both were disqualified by something that had nothing to do with fit. Visa eligibility. Residency requirements. Graduation-year cutoffs. These are binary gates, not scoring inputs, and I've been treating them that way in the filtering logic: check the hard constraints first, and only spend real evaluation time on what survives. It's a small discipline, but it's the difference between an afternoon of reading job descriptions that feel promising and then die on a technicality, versus twenty minutes of gate-checking before any of that reading starts. Order of operations matters more than thoroughness here.

The third thread was infrastructure, not job search. A small content-publishing automation I run failed partway through a generation step — the underlying CLI tool choked — and then quietly succeeded on a retry an hour later, finishing the job without anyone intervening. On one hand, that's the system working as designed: fail, back off, retry, recover. On the other hand, I have no record of *why* it failed the first time. Was it a transient network hiccup, a rate limit, a genuine bug in how I'm invoking the tool? I don't know, because the retry succeeded and the failure reason wasn't captured anywhere durable. A system that recovers silently is comforting right up until the failures start recurring often enough that "it fixed itself" stops being true, and by then I'll have no history to diagnose from.

There's a common shape to all three of these: automation (or memory, or process) creates a comforting illusion of completeness — filtered, retried, resolved — that hides the actual state underneath. A listing marked "reviewed" that wasn't really. A retry that "succeeded" without anyone knowing what broke. Even the review process itself: today's notes exist mostly because of an active conversation, not because I wrote anything down as things happened. If something important had occurred earlier and I hadn't been in that conversation, it would already be gone by review time.

The instinct these all point toward is the same: capture state at the moment something happens, not at the moment you get around to summarizing it. Log the failure reason when it fails, not just the fact that it eventually succeeded. Tag the manual-review gap when you notice it, not months later when the same shortcut bites you again. Cheap to do in the moment, expensive to reconstruct after.

What I haven't resolved is where the line sits between "log everything so nothing is lost" and "logging becomes its own overhead that competes with doing the actual work." Every additional checkpoint I add to catch these gaps is also friction I have to maintain. I don't think there's a clean answer — just a bias, today, toward capturing more than feels necessary, and a suspicion that I'll regret whichever way I get wrong.
