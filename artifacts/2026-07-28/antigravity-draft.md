title: "What a Missing Meeting Link Taught Me About Trusting My Own Systems"
date: 2026-07-28
description: "Notes from a day of job-search logistics, a utility bill audit, and a personal automation pipeline that quietly stopped writing to itself — and what they have in common."
tags: ["reflection", "systems", "job-search", "automation", "personal-infrastructure"]

Today had three unrelated threads — an interview, a utility bill, and a broken cron job — and by the evening I realized they were all teaching the same lesson from different angles: the gap between "the system ran" and "the system worked" is where all my actual risk lives.

**The interview logistics near-miss.** I had a mid-morning interview, and about ten minutes before it started I discovered the calendar invite, the original email, and the `.ics` file all lacked a video meeting link. Three independent sources of truth, and all three had the same hole in them. Nothing malicious, just an oversight upstream — but it meant I was scrambling to reach the recruiter at the worst possible moment instead of during a calm afternoon the day before.

The fix isn't complicated: when an interview invite lands, check the `LOCATION` field, the video-conferencing field, and the `.ics` attachment immediately, not the morning of. But the deeper point is that redundancy in sources doesn't protect you if all the sources were generated from the same upstream mistake. Three copies of a missing fact are still a missing fact. I've started treating "I have it in three places" as a weaker signal than I used to — it tells you about resilience to corruption, not about correctness at the source.

**The evidence-layering habit.** Later, digging into a prospective employer and a possible future interviewer, I kept running into public information that was suggestive but not confirmed — a name that fit the role, a headcount estimate that was really just an estimate stitched together from scattered listings. The temptation is to round these up to facts because they're convenient and because you want to walk into the next conversation feeling prepared. I made a point of keeping them explicitly labeled as unconfirmed rather than letting them harden into assumptions I'd act on. It's a small discipline — tag your inferences as inferences — but it's the difference between "I did my homework" and "I did my homework and then quietly lied to myself about how solid it was."

**The utility bill decision.** I also went back and re-audited a decision to switch energy providers, because a naive year-over-year bill comparison made the switch look worse than it was. Once I compared *current rates from both providers*, accounting for a competitor's recent price increase, the original decision held up fine. This is a pattern I keep re-learning: comparing a past bill to a current bill isn't a comparison of the decision, it's a comparison of two different points in a moving system. If you want to evaluate a choice, you have to reconstruct the counterfactual at the same point in time, not just diff two invoices and call it analysis.

**The automation that stopped, quietly.** The thread that actually unsettled me was discovering that my own daily memory-logging pipeline — the thing that's supposed to write a dated log file and promote important facts into long-term storage — hadn't run cleanly for two days. Not because it crashed loudly. It just... didn't complete. Tool calls executed, preparation happened, and then the final write silently failed to land, apparently because of an unstable model response on a scheduled job with no verification step behind it. I only noticed because I went looking for a specific file and it wasn't there.

That's the uncomfortable part. A cron job that fails loudly is a minor annoyance. A cron job that fails silently and produces no artifact is a trust problem, because you don't find out until you need something that should have been captured and wasn't. The fix I'm reaching for is to bind anything load-bearing to a model/config combination that's actually been tested against the real scheduled scenario, not just tested manually once, and to add some kind of "did this actually write a file" check rather than assuming completion from "the job started."

What ties all three together: in each case, the failure wasn't in the hard part. The interview prep, the provider research, the bill math, the memory architecture — those were all fine. The failure was in the boring connective tissue: a field that didn't get filled in, a rate that didn't get re-fetched, a write that didn't get confirmed. I don't think this is a personality flaw so much as a structural one — the boring connective tissue is exactly the part that's least interesting to check, and therefore the part most likely to be silently broken.

I still don't have a satisfying answer for how much verification overhead is reasonable to bolt onto every small automated or semi-automated process in a life. Check everything and you've built a second job auditing the first job. Check nothing and you find out your memory system has had a two-day hole in it only by accident. I don't know where the line is, and I suspect it moves depending on how much you're relying on any given system — which means I'll probably get it wrong again before I get it right.
