---
title: "When 'Resolved' Isn't the Same as 'Verified'"
date: 2026-08-14
description: "Notes on the gap between fixing a problem and confirming it's actually fixed, drawn from a day of chasing down a billing failure, a suspicious login, and a noisy alert."
tags: ["reflection", "systems", "job-search", "monitoring"]
---

Today had three separate threads that all converged on the same underlying lesson: the moment something *looks* resolved is not the moment it *is* resolved, and conflating the two is where I keep losing time.

The clearest example was a utility bill. An automatic payment had failed — dishonoured, in the polite banking term — and my first instinct was the obvious one: top up the account balance and assume the original charge would just retry itself. It doesn't work that way. A dishonoured payment doesn't sit in a queue waiting for funds to appear; the retry logic (if it exists at all) is the provider's decision, not mine. Fixing my side of the equation didn't fix the transaction. I ended up paying manually, plus a dishonour fee, and now I'm sitting in a genuinely uncomfortable state: paid, but not yet confirmed cleared, with a real risk of either double-paying if I get impatient or missing a second failure if I don't check back. The correct move, in hindsight, was to check the provider's own status page or app *before* acting, not after. "I addressed the cause" and "the effect is resolved" are different claims, and only one of them is verifiable without waiting.

The second thread was a login alert from an account platform, flagging access from a location I don't recognize. This is the same pattern wearing a security costume: an alert fired, and the temptation is to treat the alert itself as the event requiring action, when actually the alert is just a prompt to go verify whether there's a real event underneath it. I haven't closed this one out yet — it's sitting as an open item, which is the honest state to leave it in rather than assuming it's fine because nothing else looks disturbed.

The third thread was about a monitoring bot that sends a daily report on background job activity. Today's report had zero anomalies, and it was still styled as an alert — same visual weight as a real incident. That's a design bug, not a monitoring bug: a system that cries wolf in tone even when it has nothing to report trains you to stop reading it closely. The fix is obvious once named — status reports and alerts need visually distinct formats, because the whole point of an alert is that it interrupts a pattern of calm. If "calm" and "alarm" render identically, you've deleted the signal.

There was a fourth, quieter thread underneath a job search I've been running semi-automatically: a scoring system that ranks postings using keyword matching, feeding into a queue for human review. Today's review caught the same failure mode I keep having to catch — broad terms like "AI" or "automation" or "product" inflate a score without saying anything about whether the role is real, local, or attainable. One posting scored high automatically and dropped nearly ten points after actually reading what the day-to-day work involved versus what the title implied. The keyword layer is useful as a first filter and actively misleading as a final judgment. I don't think the fix is a better keyword list — I think the fix is treating the automated score as a *sort order for attention*, never as a verdict, and that's a distinction I have to keep re-learning rather than one I've internalized.

If there's a thread tying all four together, it's this: automation is good at doing the mechanical half of a task and bad at knowing when the mechanical half is actually done. The utility payment, the login alert, the noisy status report, and the job-scoring pipeline all failed at the same seam — the handoff point where a system needs a human (or a second system) to confirm that the intended real-world state has actually been reached, not just that an action was taken. Building the action is the easy 80%. Building — or personally performing — the verification step is the part I keep underinvesting in, probably because it doesn't feel like progress in the moment. Sending the payment feels like doing something. Waiting two business days to confirm it cleared feels like doing nothing, even though it's the step that actually closes the loop.

I don't have a clean system for closing that gap yet. Every fix I make today is local — check this one status page, add a visual distinction to this one bot, discount this one keyword. What I don't have is a general habit that reliably converts "I took the action" into "I confirmed the outcome" without me having to consciously remember to do it every single time, for every single system. Maybe that habit doesn't scale and the honest answer is that verification is supposed to feel like separate, deliberate work — but that sits uneasily with how much of what I'm trying to automate exists specifically so I don't have to keep doing deliberate work.
