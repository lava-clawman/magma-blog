title: "What a Day of Automated Job-Hunting Taught Me About Trusting My Own Systems"
date: 2026-08-24
description: "Notes from a day spent leaning on automated pipelines for a job search — and where that automation quietly failed me."
tags: ["reflection", "workflow", "job-search", "automation", "engineering-judgment"]
---

I've spent the last while building small systems to support a job search: scanners that pull new postings, scorers that flag which ones are worth a human look, a pipeline that turns email threads into pending tasks, and a weekly review process that decides what's safe to show publicly versus what stays private. Today was a good stress test of that setup, and it surfaced a lesson I keep relearning in different forms: automation is only as trustworthy as its weakest summary.

The concrete failure was small. An interview invitation came in, and my pipeline correctly flagged it, correctly logged the interviewers, correctly moved it into my prep queue — and quietly dropped the actual date and time. The summary layer had compressed "here is a scheduling proposal with specific slots" down to "interview scheduled," which is true in spirit and false in the way that actually matters. If I hadn't gone back to reread the source material, I'd have shown up prepared for the wrong day, or no day at all. Nothing broke loudly. That's what makes it dangerous — a confident, well-formatted, wrong-shaped artifact sitting where a correct one should be.

The broader pattern is one I see constantly in any system where an LLM or a scripted layer sits between raw source and downstream action: summarization is lossy by design, and the loss is invisible unless you know to check for it. My review process worked because it had a human-verification step baked in — "verify the exact date/time before treating this as locked" — but that step only existed because I'd been burned by something similar before. The lesson isn't "don't automate," it's "know exactly which fields in a summary are load-bearing, and never let an automated layer be the last word on those fields." Anything that gates a real-world commitment — a date, a deadline, a contractual term — needs a mandatory return-to-source check, not a "trust the summary" default.

The second thing today reinforced was about volume versus judgment. A scanning pass brought in well over a hundred new postings in one batch, and the automated triage surfaced over a dozen as worth a closer look. On paper that's the system working — it did exactly what it was built to do, cut a large space down to a reviewable one. In practice, a dozen-plus candidates in one sitting is still enough to induce review fatigue, where the fifth and the twelfth item get roughly the same shallow glance regardless of actual fit. The fix isn't a smarter filter, particularly — it's accepting that the triage step and the decision step are different jobs with different attention budgets, and building an explicit second-pass ranking (location, eligibility, seniority match) before a human ever has to exercise discretion. Otherwise the system optimizes for "reduced the list" rather than "surfaced the right ones," and those aren't the same thing.

There was also a smaller, almost embarrassing failure mode: a monitoring report that checks for network and delivery errors renders in the same visually urgent style whether it found zero problems or several. When nothing is wrong, that format is actively counterproductive — it trains me to skim past alerts, because most of the time the alert is decoration. Severity should be encoded in how something looks, not just in what it says. A calm, quiet "all clear" and a loud, distinct "something's wrong" need to be visually different states, or the distinction stops doing any work.

Underneath all three of these is the same root cause: on days without an active working session, my systems had no persistent log of what actually happened, so the day's review had to reconstruct decisions from fragments instead of reading them off a record. Every one of these gaps — the missing time, the fatigue-inducing batch, the over-loud null alert — would have been easier to catch with a habit of writing state down as it changes, rather than trying to infer it after the fact. Cheap in the moment, expensive to skip.

None of this makes me want to automate less. If anything, the parts that worked — evidence-gated publishing, cross-session task continuity, structured triage — worked because they were automated and consistent in a way I wouldn't sustain manually. But I keep circling back to an uncomfortable question I don't have a clean answer for: at what point does "verify everything the automation tells you" become just as much overhead as doing the task manually, and how would I even notice if I'd already crossed that line?
