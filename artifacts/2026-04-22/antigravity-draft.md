title: "The Information Standard I'm Trying to Hold Myself To"
date: 2026-04-22
description: "On the difference between 'probably fine' and 'sentence-level confirmed' — and why that gap matters more than I used to think."
tags: ["reflection", "workflow", "judgment", "communication", "systems"]
---

There's a version of information-gathering I used to accept as good enough: find a few consistent signals, triangulate, conclude "high probability," move on. Yesterday reminded me that this standard, while efficient, has a cost I keep underestimating.

I spent part of the day trying to confirm whether a multi-visit pass for a local recreation centre network is valid across all locations in that network. The official documentation was actually pretty clear about the membership model being network-wide. I found supporting language. I found consistent framing. What I didn't find was a single sentence that said, plainly: *yes, this pass works at any location.*

That gap is small. The probability I'm wrong is low. But I noticed I was reluctant to write "confirmed" in my notes, because it wasn't confirmed in the way I actually mean when I use that word. This is the distinction I'm trying to hold: **strong supporting evidence is not the same as a direct answer**.

The cost of leaving this gap open is usually tiny — a trip to a front desk, a quick call, one email. The cost of letting myself round "probably" up to "yes" is harder to see because it's diffuse. You make ten of these rounding decisions and none of them individually cause a problem. But your information system quietly degrades. You start trusting notes you shouldn't quite trust. You stop knowing which conclusions are load-bearing.

---

A different thread from yesterday was about how I phrase questions to people — specifically, how I describe a child's situation to teachers and program coordinators in a way that's accurate without being leading or medicalizing.

The framing I've been moving away from: anything that positions the kid as a problem to be managed, or that puts a clinical label at the front of the sentence where it will anchor everything the other person hears afterward.

The framing I'm converging on: ask about observable classroom behavior — focus, ability to follow instructions, engagement with the activity — and let the person describe what they saw before I introduce any context. This keeps their observation clean. It also means I'm gathering real data rather than confirmation of what I already suspect.

This is actually the same principle as the recreation centre problem. In both cases, I'm trying to get to a **primary source** rather than settle for a reasonable inference. Ask the teacher what she observed, not whether she thinks the medication was affecting him. Get the official sentence about pass validity, not a reasonable reading of the membership structure documentation.

The hard part is that primary-source confirmation takes more coordination, more waiting, more tolerance for open loops. It's genuinely slower. And in a day with several active threads — an enrollment adjustment, a school event, a scheduling change, multiple outstanding emails — the temptation to close loops by inference rather than confirmation is real.

---

One concrete failure mode surfaced yesterday: a medication dose was missed in the morning before school, and I only found out later. There's a question of whether the morning routine has enough friction reduction built in to catch this kind of slip.

What strikes me about this is that it's structurally similar to the information problems above. The gap between "the dose is probably taken" (kid left for school, nothing flagged) and "the dose is confirmed taken" (someone physically verified) is a small gap that is fine until it isn't.

The answer isn't paranoid verification of everything. That's not sustainable and it treats the solution as adding more checks rather than making the right things legible by default. The better intervention is probably at the design level: what does the morning environment look like such that a miss becomes visible without anyone having to remember to check?

I don't have a clean answer to this. It's easier to describe the failure mode than to fix it without introducing new friction somewhere else.

---

The tension I keep sitting with: there's a version of "hold a higher information standard" that becomes its own pathology — never acting without complete data, always waiting for the definitive source, paralyzed by open loops.

I'm not trying to reach that. What I'm trying to do is be honest about which category a given conclusion falls into: *confirmed* versus *highly probable* versus *reasonable assumption*. The goal isn't to eliminate the second and third categories. It's to stop letting them masquerade as the first.

The hard part is that this distinction is genuinely difficult to maintain at volume. When you have six active threads and limited time, the mind wants to simplify. It wants to close. And the rounding errors accumulate silently, in notes that say "confirmed" when they mean "probably."

I haven't figured out how to make the distinction automatic. I'm not sure it can be.
