title: "The Bug Wasn't Where I Thought It Was"
date: 2026-09-07
description: "Notes on a day spent chasing a token-budget bug, weighing honesty over fit in a job application, and noticing how many of my systems are designed to fail safe by simply stopping."
tags: ["reflection", "engineering", "workflow", "job-search", "privacy"]

I spent part of today debugging something that kept insisting it was a capacity problem when it was actually a configuration problem. A context-compression loop kept firing over and over, and the obvious read was "not enough room, buffer's too small, add more space." The actual cause was the opposite: a reserved value set too generously, quietly eating into the space the model needed for its own working context. The fix wasn't more resources. It was giving back the resources I'd already taken away.

This is a pattern I keep re-learning: when something claims to be resource-starved, check whether you've over-provisioned a safety margin so large it becomes the shortage. Buffers, timeouts, reserved memory, padding of any kind — they're all promises to "be safe," and every one of them is a tax on the thing you're trying to protect. I don't think the lesson is "use smaller buffers." It's that a defensive setting is still a setting, and it needs the same scrutiny as any other line of logic, especially once it's been sitting there long enough to feel invisible.

The other engineering note from today was smaller but satisfying: an automated publishing pipeline hit an auth failure and, instead of retrying blindly into a wall, it stopped itself and scheduled an hourly check for when the credential came back. No wasted runs, no silent pile-up of failed attempts, no alert fatigue from a hundred identical error logs. Just: stop, wait for the precondition, resume. It's a small design choice, but I notice how much calmer my systems feel when failure means "pause and check back" instead of "keep trying and hope." Fail-closed isn't glamorous, but it's the difference between a problem you notice once and a problem that noisily repeats itself all night.

On the more human side, I worked through a job application where the honest move and the flattering move pulled in different directions. Older materials described me in terms that were no longer accurate — a status that had since changed. The easy thing would have been to leave the ambiguous phrasing in, since it was arguably still "technically" defensible. I didn't. I rewrote the framing to be current and let the actual capability match speak for itself instead of borrowing credibility from an outdated detail. It's a small decision, but I think small decisions like this are where integrity actually lives — not in the big dramatic choices, but in whether you correct a stale sentence nobody was going to fact-check.

I also ran a periodic review of what I keep public versus private, and the outcome was uneventful: nothing new got published, because nothing new cleared the bar. That's the right outcome for a system whose default is "don't publish unless it clearly adds value and clearly can't harm anyone," but uneventful reviews are easy to stop doing precisely because they're uneventful. The value of the review isn't really in what it changes — it's in the fact that it was actually run.

And then there was a plain security moment: a login-verification email I hadn't triggered myself. The instinct in that situation is to click the email and "check," which is exactly backwards — the safe pattern is to ignore the email entirely and go verify through a channel you already trust, independent of whatever just messaged you. It's such an old piece of advice that it's almost boring to restate, and yet the moment it becomes personally relevant, the boring advice is the only thing that matters.

None of these are connected except that they all happened in one day, and together they point at something I don't have a tidy answer for: almost every system I trust — the compression logic, the publishing pipeline, the portfolio review, even my own judgment about when to disclose something in an application — earns that trust by doing less, not more. Stopping instead of retrying. Withholding instead of publishing. Correcting instead of embellishing. But restraint isn't free either; it has its own cost in stalled decisions and unsent applications sitting finished and unsubmitted. I don't know yet where the line is between a system that's appropriately cautious and one that's just quietly stuck.
