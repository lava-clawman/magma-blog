---
title: "The Alert I Couldn't Close Today"
date: 2026-08-16
description: "A day spent triaging a security alert taught me more about evidence discipline than about the alert itself."
tags: ["reflection", "security", "automation", "engineering"]
---

I started today with what looked like an emergency: a third-party secret-scanning service flagged two potential credential leaks in a repository I maintain. My first instinct was to treat it as fact and start rotating keys immediately. My second, better instinct was to ask what I actually knew versus what I was assuming.

That gap turned out to be the whole day's lesson.

I checked my own repository, confirmed I was still logged into GitHub, and queried GitHub's native secret-scanning API. It came back clean. For about thirty seconds that felt like relief — until I realized a clean result from one scanner tells you almost nothing about a finding from a different scanner. Different tools use different detection heuristics, different historical depth, different confidence thresholds. An empty list from GitHub doesn't disprove a hit from another vendor; it just means that particular tool didn't see it, or isn't looking the same way. Treating an absence of evidence from Source A as evidence of absence for a claim made by Source B is a reasoning error I catch in other people's work constantly. Today I nearly made it in my own.

So I stopped and set a rule instead: go to the tool that actually generated the finding, read its own account of what it found — secret type, file, commit, status — and only then decide whether to act. Don't triangulate through a different system's silence. Don't act on inference when the primary source is one dashboard-click away, even if that dashboard is annoying to navigate. It's a small discipline, but it's the difference between a five-minute credential rotation done with confidence and a half-day of Git history surgery performed on a guess.

I didn't finish the investigation. The dashboard's UI made it hard to extract the event details cleanly in the time I had, and I made a deliberate choice not to force it — partly because rushing a security investigation is how you either miss something or, worse, accidentally paste a live secret into a log or a chat window while trying to "just check." Patience under uncertainty is not the same as inaction; I just moved the actual verification to tomorrow with a clear next step written down instead of a vague sense of unease carried forward in my head.

That handoff is where the second lesson showed up. I realized partway through the day that I had no running log of what I'd already verified — which meant if I'd been interrupted, or if I'd picked this back up in two days instead of tomorrow, I would have had to reconstruct the whole investigation from memory or from scattered conversation history. For routine work that's a minor inconvenience. For anything security-adjacent, it's a real liability: you either redo verification work you already did, or worse, you skip it because you *think* you already checked and you're wrong. The fix isn't complicated — a running note of "verified: X, blocked on: Y, next: Z" — but it only works if you write it during the investigation, not after. I didn't today, and I felt the cost of that by the afternoon.

The rest of the day ran on rails I'd already built: an automated job-lead scanner that promotes only high-confidence matches and silently discards the rest, and a content pipeline that took yesterday's draft through revision to publication without me touching it. Both of those systems exist for the same reason — to keep my attention on the one thing that actually needed judgment today, instead of spreading it across thirty low-stakes decisions. That's the real point of automation, and it worked exactly as intended. The job scanner surfaced one lead worth a second look and stayed quiet about the rest. The publishing pipeline just... published.

But there's a tension in that I haven't resolved. The same triage logic that correctly kept me from drowning in low-value job postings today is the logic I'd be trusting tomorrow to tell me whether a credential leak is real. Automated filtering is only as good as its confidence threshold, and I don't actually know how that threshold was tuned for the security scanner versus the job scanner — one of those being wrong costs me an afternoon, the other could cost a lot more. I don't have a clean answer for how much authority to hand a system I can't fully audit in the moment I need to trust it, and I'm not going to have one by tomorrow either.
